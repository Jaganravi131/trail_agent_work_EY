How to Sleep After a Night Shift (the 90-Minute Rule, Not 8 Hours of Lying There)

Alt text is the actual image description here — replace with a real photo of your own setup. Original photos of real bedrooms outperform stock on this topic for trust and cost nothing.

You get home at 07:45, you are destroyed, and you cannot sleep. That combination is not a personal failing and it isn't a mystery. There are three specific reasons it happens, and all three are fixed in the 90 minutes before you get into bed — not in bed itself.

Why you can't sleep after a night shift

Three mechanisms, and they stack:

1. Your body clock is pointed the wrong way. Your circadian pacemaker is set almost entirely by light at the eye. After a night of work you've usually accumulated several hours of bright artificial light, plus daylight on the drive home and the walk in. At 08:00 your brain has a strong, recent instruction that it is morning and the day is starting. Sleep pressure is high; the clock is overriding it.

2. Caffeine you had at 03:00 is still working. Caffeine's elimination half-life in a healthy adult is commonly given at around 5 hours, with a real-world range of roughly 2–10. A 200 mg dose at 03:00 means about 100 mg still circulating at 08:00 and around 50 mg at 13:00. Falling asleep anyway is not proof of no effect: caffeine measurably reduces deep sleep and increases light sleep even in people who report falling asleep normally, and it also shifts the circadian clock later — the exact opposite of what you need. In one well-known study, 400 mg taken 0, 3 and 6 hours before bedtime all still disrupted sleep, including the 6-hour condition where participants felt fine. (Drake et al., Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, 2013)

3. You arrive home and start doing things. The drive, the bin, the washing machine, the phone, the text back, the "quick look" at the news, a bowl of cereal standing up. Two hours pass, you've dozed on the sofa without sleeping, and the sleep window has closed. Alertness partially recovers — that's the second wind people mistake for "not being tired."

The first is why timing beats technique. The second is why your cut-off time matters more than your mattress. The third is the one you can fix today.

The 90-minute rule

Treat the 90 minutes after clocking out as a fixed sequence. Nothing in it is optional and nothing in it is not about getting to bed.

StepWhenWhat
1Leaving the buildingSunglasses on before you go outside, not after you're in the car. No coffee stop on the way home.
2Before you need itCurtains/foil/blind already closed, fan on, heating off, phone charging outside the room, alarm for the wake time set. Do this at 07:30, not at 08:40 when you're in bed bargaining with yourself.
307:50Straight in, straight to a 4-minute warm shower. No "sitting down for a second" on the sofa — for most people that is where the night is lost.
408:00Small savoury snack: yoghurt, eggs, toast and cheese. Already out of the fridge, nothing cooked. Roughly 200–300 calories, 15–20 g protein.
508:20Bed. Clock turned away. If you're awake past ~20 minutes, out of the room, dim light, something boring and physical, back when sleepy.
6WakeFixed time, alarm, even after a bad night. Then 10 minutes of bright light immediately.

Step 6 is the one that feels wrong and is the one that works. If a bad night slides your wake from 12:30 to 16:00, you will not nap at 16:00, you will not sleep after the next shift, and you'll be 30 hours awake by night two. The wake time is the anchor, not the bedtime. Fix the wake time and the bedtime becomes trainable.

The 3-2-1-0 countdown

Counting back from your target lights-out. This is the whole system in four numbers, and it's what I'd do if I only ever did one thing.

  • 3 hours before bed — last real food. A big meal at 05:00 asks a gut that believes it's midnight to do a daytime job; reflux and a heavy stomach are common reports. Small and savoury at the end. See what to eat on a night shift.
  • 2 hours before bed — last caffeine. That's the floor, not the target; 6 hours is where the research on disruption actually sits. Most people find that moving their last dose to around 4 hours before shift end fixes more than any purchase. The caffeine arithmetic for nights is here.
  • 1 hour before bed — lights down. Warm and dim inside, and on a morning commute you can't avoid the sun, so you block it instead.
  • 0 — no clock. No arithmetic about hours remaining. It is the single most reliable way to stay awake, and it's voluntary.

"But I only get four hours"

Then plan four hours and stop fighting for eight.

For someone on 4-on-4-off nights in a bright house, 4.5 to 5.5 hours is often the honest maximum in one block. Forcing eight hours of bed does two things: you learn to associate the bed with wakefulness — a real conditioned response, and the usual way shift workers develop chronic insomnia on top of the shift work — and you spend two hours doing sums in the dark.

A structure that works better for a lot of people:

  • 4.5 hours core sleep immediately after the shift (three 90-minute cycles, which is why the number is 4.5 and not 4).
  • A 90-minute anchor nap before the next shift, alarm on.
  • Total ~6 hours, at times your body can be trained to hit, plus a mid-shift 15–20 minute nap where the role and the safety rules allow it.

People who switch from "8 hours or failure" to that split typically report feeling better on 6 hours than they did lying on 4.5. It isn't less sleep — it's sleep that's placed where the pressure makes it possible.

The bedroom: what actually matters, in cost order

Score these before spending money. If you're failing at light and noise, no supplement or gadget fixes it.

  1. Light gaps, not light levels. Most "blackout" curtains are lined fabric with gaps at the sides and top. At pillow height, those gaps are what wakes people. Fix order: a rolled towel or draught excluder at the bottom of the door (kills hall light and street noise at once), then a duvet or blanket pinned or taped over the window, then aluminium foil matte-side out taped to the glass. Twelve minutes of manual work; blocks more than most cheap curtains. If you rent, a bit of tape residue on glass comes off with hot water and a blade.
  2. Earplugs, inserted properly. Foam plugs fail far more often from insertion than from quality: roll thin, pull the top of your ear up and back, insert, hold 20 seconds until it expands. If you've "tried them and they don't work," you probably tried them incorrectly for 30 seconds.

If the plugs are the problem rather than the noise — one ear sealed, one not, or one that falls out by 04:00 — the fitting tests and the material trade-offs are in earplugs for day sleeping.

  1. A steady noise floor beats silence. You can't control a household's daytime — lawnmowers, the bus stop, the recycling lorry — so mask it: a fan, or a rain loop on an old phone through a cheap speaker, just above the street level. Steady background swamps the transients that startle you awake; total silence makes every one of them a threat.
  2. Cool: about 18–20 °C / 65–68 °F, and cooler rather than warmer. Falling asleep requires your core temperature to fall, so heating off before bed, not after. Summer, free: freeze a bottle behind the fan, damp sheet, fewer clothes than feels sensible, sleep lower in the house because heat rises. A warm shower 60–90 minutes before bed works counterintuitively by dumping heat through your hands and feet afterwards.
  3. Then a $10–15 eye mask, if the room can't be made dark: the 3D-cup kind that doesn't press on the lids. Highest effect-to-cost ratio in the entire list for shared housing, shifts and travel. The blocker is usually a partner's feelings, which the sentence "this isn't about you, this is about how my brain works" resolves.

Blackout curtains versus a mask is a real question, so: curtains are better if you also need the room usable in the day and you can afford 60–100% of a window's cost; a mask is better if you're in shared housing, a rental you're leaving in two years, or asleep in a living room. Many people end up with a mask and a towel and spend nothing. See the curtain-vs-mask breakdown — where that page carries an affiliate link, it will say so at the top of the page.

When this isn't a sleep-hygiene problem

Stop optimising and see a clinician if any of these are true:

  • You snore, or wake gasping or choking, or you're unrefreshed no matter how much you get. Sleep apnoea is common and badly under-diagnosed in shift populations, and no curtain treats it.
  • You cannot sleep at all, in any setup, at any time, for weeks.
  • Your mood is low most days, or you've stopped enjoying things.
  • You have, or are at risk of, a circadian sleep-wake disorder — shift work sleep disorder is a real diagnosis with a code, and it is not something you brute-force with a spreadsheet.
  • You take medication with timing-dependent absorption or interactions. Ask your pharmacist; it's free and it takes ninety seconds.

And the rule that overrides everything here: never drive when you're drowsy. You cannot reliably self-assess sleepiness, because the part of the brain that would notice is the part that's asleep. Drowsy driving impairs reaction time and judgement in ways comparable to alcohol, without a test and without gradual awareness. If the shift went badly and you're wrecked: safe stop, 200 mg caffeine, 20 minutes with an alarm, then reassess. That buys maybe half an hour — it is a mitigation, not a fix.
