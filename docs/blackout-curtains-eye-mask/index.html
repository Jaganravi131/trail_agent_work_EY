Blackout curtains or an eye mask?

This is the question with no universal answer, so the useful version is a decision, not a recommendation: what your room is, what you're allowed to change, and how dark you actually need it.

One thing first. There is no study showing that buying blackout curtains makes people sleep longer. What's known is narrower and still useful: light reaching the eye in the morning hours pulls a body clock the wrong way and thins sleep, so a dark room helps you stay asleep when the sun is up. That's why this page is about measuring a room, not about a product. The number this site uses is a dim bedroom — dark enough that you can't read by it lying down. Under about 5 lux at pillow height is the shorthand; the read-by-it test is what you can actually check.

The 20-minute free test, before any money

Do this tonight-or-tomorrow, not next weekend:

  1. At the brightest hour your sleep overlaps — usually 11:00 to 14:00 — close the curtains as they are.
  2. Lie down in the sleep position. Look at the wall behind the bed, then at the ceiling, then at the gap where the curtain meets the window frame.
  3. Try to read a phone screen at the lowest brightness. If the room lets you read it comfortably, it's too bright.
  4. Take one photo from the pillow, no flash, phone on auto. A bright photo with visible detail means light is entering the room, not just the gap you'd expect.

Now block everything cheap and repeat the test: cardboard cut to the window frame, aluminium foil facing the glass, taped at the edges with parcel tape; a towel rolled and jammed under the door; a folded dark towel along the top of the curtain rail. If the room goes properly dark with cardboard, your problem is a gap or a thin fabric, and a $12 mask or a $0 window blind might beat $120 of curtains. If the light still comes through the fabric, you need mass and overlap, which is the curtains-and-liner answer.

The cardboard is not a joke solution. Plenty of shift workers keep it: it blocks more light than most curtains, costs nothing, is renter-safe with tape that peels, and can go up in 20 minutes.

Buying curtains: the four things that decide it

  1. Oversize. Curtain fabric needs to extend past the window frame by roughly 10–15 cm on each side and reach 2–5 cm past the sill or puddle slightly on the floor. Curtains the exact size of the window leak light at every edge, and that leak is the entire problem.
  2. Weight and lining. Two or three layers with a dense backing beats a single polyester sheet at the same price. Hold a panel against a lamp: if you see the filament, you'll see the sun.
  3. The top gap. Eyelets hang off a rod with a lit slot along the top — the most common reason a "blackout" curtain fails. A pole with a pocket, or a return bend that brings the fabric to the wall, fixes it. Rings with clip-on tape and a board above the rail also work.
  4. Claimed percentages. "Blocks 75%" and "blocks 99%" are fabric-laboratory numbers measured on a flat sample with no window frame, no gaps and no sky behind it. They are not a promise about your room. Two curtains with the same claim can perform completely differently in the same window, which is why the photo test from the pillow is worth more than the spec sheet.

If you're in shared housing or a place you'll leave in two years, skip the curtains, and buy the mask plus a $6 tension-blind cut to frame size. The room stays usable for everyone else, and you take both with you.

Buying a mask: what actually matters

  • 3D / contoured cups, so nothing presses the lids. Flat masks feel fine for ten minutes and unbearable at hour three, and pressing on the eyes is a poor night's start for no benefit.
  • Adjustable strap with no buckle at the back of the head if you sleep on your side or front.
  • Nose bridge fit — most light leaks down the front, not around the sides. A mouldable bridge or a stitched nose dart fixes it; a straight cut doesn't.
  • Something you'll wash. Weekly, by hand, mild soap; the elastic and the foam die faster than the fabric, so replacing every 3 to 6 months is sensible rather than scientific. A mask that smells is a mask you'll stop wearing, and then the room is still bright.
  • For a nap rather than a whole day, a mask is worth more per pound than a curtain: the 20-minute nap page covers the timing that goes with it.

The order to spend in

  1. Blackout the window with cardboard or a cut-to-size blind. Free, reversible, testable tonight.
  2. Mask. Cheapest per unit of darkness, works anywhere you sleep.
  3. Door draft excluder and a folded towel at the bottom — hallway light is a common culprit nobody measures.
  4. Curtains, when you want the room usable in the day, or you're heating the place in winter and want the free side benefits.
  5. An eye and ear solution together if the household, not the sun, is waking you: a steady noise floor swamps transients better than thicker fabric does — that part is in the main sleep guide.
