4 on, 4 off Night Shifts: the Sleep Schedule That Survives the Handover

Four nights on and four off is one of the better patterns a night worker can have — and almost everybody runs it badly, because they treat the four off as four days of freedom rather than as part of the schedule. The block is the unit. Get the boundaries right and the middle takes care of itself; get them wrong and you spend two of your four days off recovering, which is the actual complaint behind "I have no life on nights."

This page is the arithmetic for the boundaries. For a single night in isolation, read sleep after a night shift and the pre-shift nap.

What four nights actually does to a clock

One night of work costs you a partial shift of circadian time, and you lose most of what you gained the first morning you sleep until 11:30 instead of 08:30. Across four nights, something more useful happens: if you hold the wake time and get 10 minutes of bright light on the way home, your clock drifts toward nocturnal by roughly 1 hour a night. Say 3 to 3.5 hours of movement over the block — real, and completely reversible. You lose most of it inside the first 36 hours off if you sleep late twice. That's not full adaptation — full adaptation takes weeks and a blackout lifestyle — but it's enough that nights two, three and four feel markedly better than night one.

Then the four off arrive, and everything you gained is spent going back. That's not a failure; it's the trade the pattern offers. The only real decision is how much of the shift you keep through the off block, and that decision has three answers with different prices.

Three schedules, by what you're willing to pay

A. Hold the nocturnal block (best recovery, worst social life). Bed 09:00–15:00, asleep by 15:30, up 20:00, out in the evening, nothing scheduled before 18:00. You feel almost normal for the whole four off, and you start night one fresh. The cost: you are unavailable for the entire working day, every working day, and if you have kids at school that's a hard no.

B. The bridge (what I'd actually recommend for most people). Nights 1–4 as usual, then on the first day off you sleep the full post-shift block and stop pushing it later. Two anchored hours in the afternoon, one long sleep at night, drift back over two days, and you're diurnal by day three of the off block. You pay for it on the first and last day of the off block and you keep the weekend. Roughly 12 hours of clock movement gets spent and re-borrowed every cycle, and you decide in advance which two days feel rubbish. This is the version in the table below.

C. Flip back immediately (cheapest on paper, most expensive in practice). "Sleep all day Saturday and be normal by Sunday" is a nice plan that survives about one weekend. Two nights of 11:00–02:00 broken sleep, and now you start night one with a clock that has moved 6 hours in the wrong direction and back. People on plan C report the exact same sentence every time: "the first night of every block is brutal." It is optional. The schedule causes it.

Nine times out of ten, the person struggling on a 4-on-4-off isn't struggling on the four nights. They're struggling on day 4 and day 8 — the two handovers, where the clock is being asked to change direction with no transition plan.

If you have to be at school gates and birthday parties

The version that works with a family, at the cost of some of the recovery: keep one protected block. Bed 09:00–14:30 on days off — 5.5 hours, dark room, fan, phone in the kitchen, room cool enough that the duvet isn't the problem (18–20 °C is the usual target) — then be up and present for the afternoon and evening, and let the night sleep come at 23:30. That's an anchor, not a schedule, and it's the difference between "I saw my kids every day of the four off" and "I slept through my days off and felt guilty about it."

One practical detail with real leverage: blackout the room you sleep in during the day properly. Tape around the edges, not just curtains. If you can read a page at pillow height, it is bright enough to hold a clock in place, which is the opposite of what you're trying to do — a light-meter app on a phone will tell you in ten seconds, and "under about 5 lux" is the target.

Rotation direction is not a preference, and you can ask for it

If your rota rotates — nights into mornings into lates — the direction matters more than almost anything on this page. Forward rotation (late → night → early) moves with your clock, at roughly 1–1.5 hours of shift per day absorbed. Backward rotation (early → night) moves against it, costs about twice as much, and the "one good night then I'm fine" feeling on the first nights block is borrowed from the last three.

You may not be able to choose. What you can do is ask, in writing, with a reason a scheduler can act on — and treat the rule of thumb for what it is: roughly 1 day of adjustment per hour of shift moved forward, with no discount for the shorter step when the rota moves the other way. Two sentences that work better than a medical plea: "I'm on backward rotation; if there's a forward-rotating line I can swap into, I'll take it. The first week after a backwards flip is when I'm most likely to make an error." That's a safety statement about the rota, not a request for special treatment, and the second version is the one that gets remembered in the staffing meeting.

If you have a diagnosed condition that affects scheduling, that's a different conversation with a different process — occupational health or whatever your employer has — and it isn't something a blog post can help you with.

Two failure modes worth naming

The "catch up on the weekend" reflex. Sleeping 09:00–19:00 on day off number two is 10 hours in bed, not 10 hours of sleep — most people get 7.5 of those and a wake-up at 15:00 they can't fall back from. It buys you one bad night before the block starts. If you're that deep in debt, take two 90-minute naps on the day rather than one marathon: same sleep, no stolen night.

Treating the whole off block as one long recovery. Four off is a week, not a convalescence. Put the one thing you actually want to do — the appointment, the DIY, the birthday — on day 6 or 7, not day 5. Day 5 is a transition, and transitions are where plans die.

Stop optimising the roster and get it looked at if

  • You're dozing off unintentionally, including behind the wheel, at any point in the block.
  • You cannot sleep 08:30–13:00 in a dark, cool room, on nights or days off, for weeks.
  • You snore and wake unrefreshed — apnoea is common, under-diagnosed in shift workers, and no schedule fixes it.
  • Your mood is low across the whole off block rather than just tired.
  • The roster is hurting your safety at work and no amount of scheduling resolves it. That's an occupational-safety matter, not a personal sleep failure.
  • You're thinking about any medication or supplement to shift your timing. Ask a pharmacist or clinician first, with your own medication list in hand; this site gives no dosing plans for anything, deliberately.

If your rota isn't 4-on-4-off, the same two-handover arithmetic — including when a flip is mathematically impossible and holding is the correct answer — is in days off after nights.

One rule outranks every schedule on this page: never drive when you're drowsy. You can't self-assess it reliably, because the part of the brain that would notice is the part that's asleep. 200 mg of caffeine and a 20-minute nap buy about 30 minutes of mitigation, which is enough to get yourself to a safe stop and nowhere near enough to make a 40-mile drive home after night four.
