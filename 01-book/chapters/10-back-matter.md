# About this book

**On the author.** J. Arek Thorne is a systems writer who has worked nights across healthcare support, overnight logistics and 24/7 monitoring, and who spent several years badly asleep before building the sequence in these pages. This book is written from that experience plus public guidance from sleep and occupational-health research, translated into decisions a person can make in a break room while tired.

It is deliberately **not** written by a clinician, and it makes no clinical claims. That's not false modesty — it's the reason the advice here is conservative and the safety rules are absolute. Where you need a clinician, the book says so, in chapter 1 and again in chapter 6, and a pharmacist can answer medication-timing questions for free in under two minutes.

If you are choosing between this book and a textbook, buy the textbook. If you are choosing between this and another 90 minutes of scrolling at 3 a.m., this is the better trade.

**Correction policy.** This manuscript is versioned in public. Errors and useful objections are logged at the book's free resources page, linked below. If you tell me something here conflicts with your employer's protocol or your professional standard, that's a correction I want.

## If this helped

Three things, in order of usefulness, all free to you:

1. **A review on Amazon**, with one specific detail — which rule you adopted, and what changed. Books like this live or die on reviews that mention *mechanisms*, because a prospective reader who works nights and is tired at 8 a.m. is scanning for "did this work for someone with my roster." Two sentences, honestly written, is worth more than five hundred vague stars.
2. **Tell one colleague** who is worse off than you were.
3. **Send me what's missing.** Which chapter didn't apply to your role? Warehouse, security, transport and acute-care reality differ enormously and I want the next edition to reflect it.
