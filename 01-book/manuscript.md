Read chapter 1. Then set five numbers and start tonight: chapters 2 through 6 each hand you one. Ignore the rest. Run the 28-day protocol in chapter 8 with the log in chapter 9, and add the other material as you need it.

Here is the compressed version, so you can start before you finish chapter 1.

### The five numbers

| # | Number | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **3-2-1-0** | Hours before sleep: last food 3, last caffeine 2 or ideally 6, lights down 1, zero clock-watching at 0. |
| 2 | **30 g** | Protein at your anchor meal, the one meal you eat at the same clock time every shift. |

| 4 | **10 min** | Bright light in your first hour on shift. The cheapest alertness tool you have, and it's free. |
| 5 | **4.5 + 1.5** | Core sleep plus anchor nap, in hours. Six hours of planned sleep beats eight hours of lying in the dark doing arithmetic. |

And one sequence, which matters more than all five:

> **Descent → Hold → Reentry.**
> Your shift is not twelve hours of suffering. It is three short problems with different solutions. Most advice fails because it treats them as one.

### What this is not

This is not a book about moral failing. If you have eaten vending-machine crackers at 3:30 a.m. for four years and told yourself the problem is your discipline, the problem is not your discipline. The problem is that you were handed a body that expects daylight and a roster that doesn't care, and then given generic advice from someone who sleeps in a bed at 10:30 p.m. in a house where nothing happens at 6 a.m.

You don't need more willpower. You need defaults that survive being exhausted. That's what's here.

This is written from years around overnight work plus public guidance from sleep and occupational-health research that I've turned into decisions you can make in a break room. Where something is a firm population-level finding — caffeine's half-life, the roughly 90-minute structure of sleep cycles, the 400 mg/day figure that food regulators commonly cite for healthy adults — it's stated plainly. # 1. The Clock You Can't See

At 4:12 a.m. on a Tuesday, in a break room that smells like burnt coffee and lemon floor cleaner, a person eats a bag of cheese crackers and a chocolate donut because it is the only thing in the building that is open. Then they go home, lie in a bed at 8:20 a.m. with the neighbours starting their weekend, and stare at the ceiling until 10:45, doing the math: *eight hours, minus what I've had, if I fall asleep now I'll get* — and that arithmetic is exactly what keeps them awake.

None of this is a character flaw. It is the predictable output of two systems that are out of phase, and once you can see both of them, most of what to do stops being willpower and starts being logistics.

## The two processes that decide how you feel

Sleep scientists describe alertness as the tug-of-war between two things. Call them the **clock** and the **pressure**.

**The clock** (experts call it Process C, the circadian system) is a roughly 24.2-hour oscillator in your brain. It is set mostly by one input: light at the eye, in the morning-ish hours. It dictates a daily curve of core temperature, hormone release, reaction time, digestion and mood. For a day worker it peaks in alertness late morning and early evening and bottoms out in the small hours. It also has a stubborn habit: without daily light cues it drifts later, which is why people can free-run on 25-hour days in bunkers.

**The pressure** (Process S, sleep pressure) is a counter. It accumulates every hour you're awake, largely independent of time of day, and drains while you sleep. Roughly: after 16–20 hours awake, a normal adult is fighting biology, not feelings.

Day workers have it easy: when pressure is at its worst, the clock says sleep, so they sleep. You don't. On a night rotation you are repeatedly asked to be alert exactly where the clock says *biological night*, and to sleep exactly where the clock says *morning, wake up, the sun is up and everyone is mowing*. The pressure might be manageable; the clock is the wall.

So the strategy is not "sleep more." It is:

1. **Move the clock deliberately** — light is the only lever with real force, and it's free.
2. **Cap the pressure** — planned naps drain the counter a little, repeatedly, instead of letting it redline.
3. **Stop feeding the wrong process** — food, caffeine and light at 3 a.m. send "it's daytime" signals to a body that must learn "it's 8 a.m. and I need to sleep now."

Everything in this book serves those three.

## Why "just sleep 8 hours" is bad advice

For someone on 4-on-4-off nights, the honest total available sleep between shifts in a bright house with kids and traffic is often 4.5 to 5.5 hours, and forcing eight hours of bed means two to three hours of awake-time in the dark. That has two costs. You learn to associate bed with wakefulness — a real conditioned response, and the single most common reason people in your situation develop chronic insomnia on top of shift work. And you start doing arithmetic in bed, which is arousal, which is the opposite of what sleep needs.

The plan in this book is instead: **protect a shorter window with ferocious consistency, and add a planned nap.** 4.5 hours of core sleep plus a 90-minute anchor nap is about six hours of actual sleep, at times your body can be trained to hit, rather than eight hours of aspirational lying there. If you're coming off a "dark room at 11 p.m. to 7 a.m. or nothing" mindset, six solid hours at a schedule will feel like a different life by day 10.

## A story about a microwave

Priya, ICU nurse, 19:00–07:30 rota, three nights then four off. She was doing everything "right": meal-prepped on her day off, four containers of chicken and rice in the fridge. By night three she was eating them cold at 5 a.m. because that's when the break finally came, was wired until noon on her drive-home adrenaline, slept 4 hours, woke at 4 p.m. feeling ill, and had a bowl of cereal and two coffees before heading back in.

We changed four things, none of them exotic.

Her anchor meal moved to 19:15, eaten in the first hour, hot, with 30 g of protein instead of 18. Her 5 a.m. food became a *snack*, not a meal, and it became whatever was within reach without a queue. Coffee: two doses, 19:30 and 23:00, then water and decaf tea after that. And the drive home got a rule: sunglasses on in the car, curtains drawn before she walked in the door — she'd start prepping the room at 6:45 rather than at 7:30 when she was already halfway to bed.

Nothing about her hours changed. No new product was bought. She slept 6 hours and 20 minutes by the second week and stopped feeling ill at 4 p.m. That's the point of a system rather than a pile of tips: small, boring, correctly-ordered changes at the right *times*, which compound, versus heroic effort at the wrong time.

## The 3-2-1-0 countdown

Write it somewhere you'll see it at 5 a.m.

Counting backwards from your target lights-out after shift:

- **3 hours** — last real food. Small, savoury, low-in-alcohol, nothing that requires your gut to work hard while your clock says it's night.
- **2 hours** — last caffeine. This is the floor, not the goal. If you can push it to 6 hours, do; that's where the research on sleep disruption actually sits. Chapter 4 does the math so you can see where you stand instead of guessing.
- **1 hour** — lights down. Screens to warm and dim. This is the "light curtain," chapter 5. On a morning commute you cannot avoid light, so you block it instead: sunglasses, and the room prepared *before* you need it.
- **0** — no clock. Turn it away. If you're awake past 20 minutes, out of bed, dim light, boring activity, back when sleepy. Not once, but every night.

That's it. If you do nothing else this month, do the countdown. It is the whole book in four numbers, and it survives being exhausted, which is the actual criterion for a rule being useful to you.

## Why this needs 28 days

Two reasons, and neither is motivational.

First, the clock moves slowly. Full circadian adaptation to a night schedule is rare in real rotating-shift populations and takes sustained daily cues — a bit per day, not one weekend. Anyone promising you a 3-day fix is describing a study with a laboratory protocol, not your life.

Second, you are not the average subject. Caffeine sensitivity varies enormously between people, as does how much food at 2 a.m. sits comfortably, and whether a split sleep pattern works for you or destroys you. Nobody can tell you your numbers from a book — including this one. Twenty-eight days of a two-minute log gives you a personal answer, and a personal answer is worth more than a month of generic ones.

Chapter 8 hands you the calendar. Chapters 2 through 6 explain why each piece is there, so you can adapt it instead of abandoning it the first week something doesn't fit.

---

**Do this now:** pick the target lights-out time for your next shift and set an alarm for 3 hours before it. Just one. That's the seed of the countdown — an alarm that says *last food*, and you'll figure out the rest of the sequence within a week.

# 2. The Descent — the three hours before you clock in

Ask most night-shift people what goes wrong and they'll answer: 4 a.m. Ask a month later and the honest answer is usually: I never got started.

The single most common failure pattern in overnight work isn't the shift itself. It's arriving on shift already 4 hours into a sleep deficit, already caffeinated past the useful point, already running on the stress of a compressed evening. Everything downstream is worse because the descent was unplanned.

Treat the three hours before you clock in as a *part of the shift*. It is the cheapest, highest-leverage block in your whole day, and unlike roster, commute and staffing ratios, you control all of it.

## The descent sequence

Anchored to a 19:00 start. Shift the whole thing by whatever your start is.

| Time | Action | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 16:00 | **Anchor nap**, 90 minutes max, alarm on, phone across the room | Drains Process S right before the longest stretch of wakefulness. 90 min ≈ one full cycle, so you wake from light sleep instead of a hole. |
| 17:40 | 10 minutes of bright light. Outside if there's any daylight left; otherwise every light you own, kitchen counter, 60 seconds | Tells the clock "evening = active," and gives you a small measured alertness bump without a drug. |
| 17:50 | **Anchor meal**, hot, 30 g protein | Chapter 3 explains why the main meal belongs at the *start* of the shift, not the middle. |
| 18:20 | Dose 1 caffeine — 100–200 mg, with food, water alongside | On the clock now, so the peak lands as you're settling into work and the tail is long gone before bed. |
| 18:35 | Bag packed, uniform out, keys, badge, lunch container on the counter | Decision removal. The version of you at 18:35 does the version of you at 03:00 a favour. |
| 18:40 | Household handoff out loud: bin, kids, "back at 08:00, don't wake me for X" | Half of all interrupted morning sleep is a preventable conversation. |
| 19:00 | On shift | |

Three things in that table are load-bearing.

## The anchor nap

Rules that make it work instead of backfiring:

- **≤90 minutes.** Longer pushes you into deep sleep and you wake up worse. If you always wake worse, cut to 60, then 45.
- **Alarm, and the alarm is not negotiable.** "I'll just rest my eyes" is how people end up sleeping 3 hours at 17:00 and being unable to sleep at 09:00, which is the death spiral of night work.
- **Consistent start time.** The nap should be at the same time relative to shift start every shift, even on a bad night. Consistency is the training.
- **Nap on the sofa or in a chair if the bed tempts you to go long.** Sleep is location-specific; you can teach your body that the couch is a 60-minute thing.
- **A "coffee nap" is the exception, not the rule**: caffeine immediately before the 20-minute nap, wake as it kicks in. See chapter 4 — use it before *hard* shifts, not daily, because it borrows from your sleep budget in a way that compounds.

If you're on a 4-on-4-off pattern, the pre-shift nap is your main sleep on the first two nights and your supplement after that. It's not a luxury; it's a designed load-bearing beam.

## The handoff conversation

Sounds trivial. It isn't.

Your morning sleep is the most fragile sleep you own, and it is frequently destroyed by other people's assumptions about what you need. The fix costs nothing and takes 40 seconds: before you leave for every shift, out loud, to whoever is awake in the house —

> I'm going to be asleep from 07:45 to about 13:30. I'll be in the bedroom, curtains closed, phone on silent. If you need me it's an emergency text, not a knock. School run is yours Tuesday, mine is yours if I'm back before 15:00.

Then write it on the fridge. Housemates and children forget what was said at 18:40; a card on the fridge with your hours on it works when memory doesn't. If you live alone, the equivalent problem is *your own* errands — the 9 a.m. delivery slot, the plumber who comes "between 8 and 12," the recycling collection. Never schedule anything in your sleep window if a human has any choice in the matter. If a delivery must be received, do it on a day off, or ask a neighbour, or let it be recollection day and accept the miss. Sleep is the asset. Deliveries are cheap.

## What to avoid in the descent

**A full gym session 40 minutes before shift start.** Moderate exercise improves sleep quality; hard exercise immediately before *sleep* delays it, and immediately before a shift leaves you shaky in hour one. If you train on shift days, put it right after waking from core sleep, or keep the pre-shift slot to a walk and stretching.

**Three coffees to "get going."** That's how people end up at dose 3 by 18:30, dose 4 at 22:00, dose 5 at 02:30, and in bed at 09:30 with a heart rate. Total caffeine matters more than any single dose. Chapter 4 does the arithmetic.

**Skipping the anchor meal because you're not hungry at 17:50.** Understandable — appetite is lowest when the clock says biological night, and early evening may be that window. So make the anchor meal small and protein-forward: three eggs, or yoghurt with a scoop of protein stirred through, or a tin of sardines on toast. Two hundred calories with 30 g of protein beats a 900-calorie meal at 4 a.m. every time, and it takes four minutes to make.

**The doom scroll.** Ninety minutes on a phone at 18:00 is the most common way a planned descent quietly becomes a rushed one. If you use your phone for the alarm, set the alarm first, then put it down and do the bag. Sequence, not self-control.

---

**Do this now:** write your next shift's descent as a five-line list with times in it — nap, light, food, first coffee, leaving. Put it where you'll see it at 16:00, not where you'll see it at 18:55, because by then it's too late to matter.

# 3. The Hold — eating between midnight and six

Nobody designs food for 3 a.m. Supermarkets are designed for the evening shop. Diet plans are designed for lunch. Break rooms are designed for a 12:30 bell that you aren't on. So you improvise with a vending machine, and then you eat like you failed.

You didn't fail. You were issued the wrong tool. Here is the right structure.

## The anchor-meal rule

**Your main meal belongs in the first hour of your shift.** Not at 4 a.m., not after work before bed, not skipped.

This feels wrong the first time you hear it, so here's the reasoning:

Digestion is under circadian control. Insulin sensitivity and gastric emptying are measurably worse in the biological night than in the biological day. A 900-calorie plate at 04:00 asks a gut that thinks it's midnight to do a daytime job, and the typical reports back are reflux, a heavy stomach, an energy collapse 90 minutes later, and worse sleep onset at 08:30 because you're still digesting.

Eat the big one at the start, when your body is in the best possible slot — you've just slept, cortisol is rising into your active phase, light is on, the meal lands before the first dip. Then, through the biological night, you graze small, savoury, high-protein things whose job is only to stop hunger from becoming a problem, not to "fuel" you.

The number to remember: **30 g of protein at the anchor meal.** Not because of gym culture. Protein is the single most reliable lever for feeling genuinely fed for six hours on a night shift, it blunts the post-meal energy slope, and it's the easiest thing to hit badly. A break-room "meal" of a sandwich and a biscuit typically delivers 14–18 g, which is why people are hungry again at 2 a.m.

## The three food decisions that matter

Don't try to eat well on a night shift. Try to make three decisions well.

**1. Anchor meal, first hour, hot, 30 g protein.** Examples: 3 eggs + 2 whites and toast; chicken thighs from Sunday's batch and microwave rice; tin of tuna, mayonnaise, sweetcorn, on bread; Greek yoghurt bowl with nuts and protein powder; leftover chilli and beans, 200 g, plus a cheese sandwich because the chilli alone is 22 g.

**2. The 2-hour snack ladder.** From hour 5 onward, eat something every 2 to 2.5 hours *only if hungry*, and pick from a pre-ranked list you never re-decide on shift:

| Tier | Option | Protein | Prep |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Yogurt tube + cheese string | 18 g | none |
| A | Handful of salted nuts + milk (UHT carton) | 12 g | none |
| A | 2 hard-boiled eggs, container in shared fridge | 12 g | 10 min weekly |
| B | Protein bar, look for ≥15 g protein and <8 g sugars | 15–20 g | none |
| B | Tuna pouch + crackers | 20 g | 30 s |
| C | Vending: peanuts > crisps > biscuits | 5–9 g | coins |
| D | Break-room leftovers nobody claimed | 0 g | shame |

Build a list you actually like in the worksheet in chapter 9, print it, tape it inside a locker or photograph it as your lock screen. A decision you've already made at 18:40 is a decision the 3 a.m. version of you doesn't have to make.

**3. Fluids.** Roughly a litre over a 12-hour shift in a climate-controlled building; more if you're on your feet in heat. Do not let a 3 a.m. headache become "I'm just old." It's usually dehydration plus caffeine withdrawal in a coat. Salted nuts and a UHT milk carton fix both.

## The slump at hour 6, and why food is the wrong lever

Around hour 6 (roughly 01:00 on a 19:00 start) and again around hour 9–10 (04:00–05:00), you hit the clock's floor. Alertness drops, hands feel clumsy, food becomes psychologically irresistible because *eating feels like an intervention*.

It mostly isn't. A biscuit gives you 40 minutes of glucose and 90 minutes of worse fatigue. The levers that work at hour 6, in descending order of effect per unit of effort:

1. **10 minutes of bright light** — walk to the lit loading bay, stand by the freezer door in the shop, put your face near the corridor LEDs. Free, fast, actual effect on the clock.
2. **10 minutes of movement** — stairs, a lap of the building, marching on the spot in the stairwell while you finish a text. Raises core temp, which is what the slump is a fall in.
3. **Cold water on the face and wrists.**
4. **Talking to a human.** Not small talk for its own sake: alertness is socially upregulated. The night porter who chats for four minutes is more awake than the one who eats a second doughnut.
5. **A 15–20 minute nap in the car or a quiet room**, if your role and safety rules allow it. This is a nap, not a sleep. Alarm. Twenty minutes. It's the most underrated tool in shift work and the one people feel most ridiculous using, which is a shame.
6. **Then food** — tier A or B from the ladder.

Note what's missing: sugary drinks. If you need the sugar, have it *with* protein and fat (peanuts and Coke beats Coke), because that flattens the slope.

## Menu templates by constraint

Not ideals. Templates for what people actually have.

### "One microwave, 20-minute break, shared fridge" (hospital, call centre, warehouse office)
Sunday batch: 1.5 kg chicken thighs, roast tray of potatoes and peppers, 400 g tin white beans. Five portions. Anchor meal = 2 portions plus a cheese string and an apple. Snack = the beans with a boiled egg. Zero morning effort. Cost per shift: whatever your normal dinner costs, because you're eating your dinner at 18:00 instead of 20:00. This is the whole trick.

### "No microwave, no fridge" (security, transport, retail)
The no-cool-bag stack: UHT milk cartons (200 ml, 7 g protein each), tuna or salmon pouches, salted peanuts in the shell, oatcakes, bread rolls, string cheese kept up to 4 hours in a bag, bananas. This is ~35 g protein per meal with no refrigeration and no equipment. Add a flask of hot water for tea — a warm drink at 3 a.m. is doing psychological work that's worth more than its calories.

### "I only get 8 minutes, and it might not come" (acute wards, emergency, line work)
Eat in one hand while you work if that's reality: a wrap with chicken and coleslaw (25 g), a yoghurt pouch, a protein shake from a carton. Then, when a break does land, spend it sitting down and looking at something far away, not eating. Rest beats nutrition in hour 9. If you have to choose between 8 minutes of food and 8 minutes of eyes-closed stillness with a break room full of noise, most of you need the stillness more than you think.

### "Days off and the double shift"
On a run of 3+ nights, do not try to eat "well." Try to eat *consistently*, and keep the anchor meal identical every shift. Variety is a daytime luxury; novelty costs decision energy you don't have at 02:00.

## Alcohol, the thing nobody raises

After a night shift: 2 units of anything with the sunrise on the window is not a wind-down, it's a second stimulant with a hangover. Alcohol suppresses the second half of the night's sleep even when it speeds the first half, and on 4 hours of sleep that turns into a shift where you are slower than you believe.

Not a moral argument, a practical one: if you drink after nights, the cheapest experiment in this book is one week of switching the post-shift drink to tea and a snack, and comparing your sleep log to the week before. Many people find this changes their sleep more than any blackout curtain does.

---

**Do this now:** pick one anchor meal you will eat before every shift for the next week and make enough of it for four portions while you're cooking for yourself anyway. No new shopping list. The recipe you already own, at 17:50, plus something to push the protein to 30 g.

# 4. Caffeine Is a Scheduler, Not a Starter

Caffeine is the most useful thing on a night shift and the most common reason a night shift person can't sleep at 08:00. Both facts come from the same property: it lasts a very long time, and it does nothing about the underlying sleep pressure.

That second part is the mental model to keep. Caffeine doesn't *reduce* tiredness. It blocks the receptors that let you *feel* it. The pressure is still there, banked, waiting for the block to wear off. Which means caffeine is not an energy source at all — it's a **timing tool**. Used as a timing tool it's excellent. Used as a substitute for sleep it's a loan at a bad rate.

## The math, in one table

Caffeine's elimination half-life in a healthy non-smoking adult is commonly given as around 5 hours, with a wide real-world range (roughly 2 to 10 hours; faster in smokers, slower in pregnancy and with some medications). You're probably not average. Assume 5, then test.

Start from **200 mg at 02:00** — a big café coffee, or a small energy drink:

| Clock | Roughly still active | What it feels like |
|---|---|---|
| 02:30 | 195 mg | Good. This is the point. |
| 07:00 | 128 mg | Fine at work. |
| 08:00, in bed | 100 mg | "I'm tired but wired." |
| 12:00 | 50 mg | Half a coffee's worth, in bed, at noon. |
| 17:00 | 25 mg | Not zero. |

One dose. Now multiply by the four or five you actually had.

This is why "I can fall asleep fine after coffee" is not evidence. Falling asleep is not the whole of sleep; caffeine measurably reduces deep sleep and increases light sleep even in people who report no trouble dropping off, and it shifts the circadian clock later, which is the exact opposite of what a night worker needs. In a well-known study, 400 mg given 0, 3 **and 6 hours** before bedtime all still disrupted sleep — including the 6-hour condition, where people *felt* unaffected. You are not a reliable instrument for measuring your own caffeine. Trust the half-life, not the feeling.

## Two doses per shift. That's the rule.

For a 19:00–07:00 shift:

- **Dose 1 — 18:50, 100–200 mg, with food.** Timed to the clock, not to the first crisis.
- **Dose 2 — 22:30–23:30, 100 mg.** Covers hour 4–6, the first slump window.
- **Then stop.** Decaf, water, milk tea, whatever. The rest of the shift is light, movement, food and naps — chapter 3's ladder — not chemistry.

Why stop so early: with lights-out near 08:30, a 23:30 dose leaves roughly 2 hours of buffer before bed at 07:00 levels — and a 02:30 dose still leaves 100 mg on board. The 23:30 cut-off is the version of the countdown's "2 hours before bed" that actually works in practice, because it also covers the *surge* at 06:00 when the handover comes and everyone else is also reaching for coffee.

If your shift is 22:00–08:00, doses go at 22:00 and 02:00, cut off by 02:30, and lights-out near 10:00.

## Amounts: what you're actually drinking

Rough figures, because real values vary by a lot — your café's "medium" may be 150 mg or 300 mg, and a 16 fl oz chain coffee can exceed 400 mg. These are usable planning numbers, not lab values.

| Source | Typical size | Caffeine |
|---|---|---|
| Instant coffee, made at work | 250 ml mug | 60–90 mg |
| Filter / drip | 350 ml | 120–200 mg |
| Espresso | single shot | 50–75 mg |
| Cold brew, iced, café | 470 ml | 180–280 mg |
| Tea, black, 3 min | 250 ml | 40–70 mg |
| Green tea | 250 ml | 20–45 mg |
| Cola | 330 ml can | 30–40 mg |
| Energy drink | 250 ml can | 80 mg |
| Energy drink | 500 ml can | 160 mg |
| Dark chocolate | 40 g | 20–35 mg |
| Pre-workout scoop | 1 scoop | 150–300 mg |
| "Just the vending machine coffee" | 180 ml | 50–70 mg |

The figure food regulators commonly cite for healthy adults is **up to 400 mg/day without increased risk**, with single doses around 200 mg. That's a ceiling, not a target, and not applicable in pregnancy (where the commonly cited figure is half or less), adolescence, or with certain medications and anxiety or arrhythmia histories. If any of those apply, this is a pharmacist conversation, not a book conversation.

Two things blow past the ceiling on night shifts without anyone noticing: pre-workout at 17:00 (200 mg) plus four coffees, and the "I'll just have another of these" energy drink at 03:00. Track total mg for one shift — the worksheet in chapter 9 — and you'll almost certainly find one dose you can delete.

## The coffee nap

The one place timing beats amount, and it's free.

1. 200 mg caffeine, fast (a coffee, a can of energy drink, two pills if you use them — pills being the cheapest dose-per-unit anywhere, and the only accurate one).
2. Immediately lie down or recline, eyes closed, alarm at 20 minutes.
3. Wake as the caffeine begins to peak.

You get the sleep-pressure reduction from the nap and the receptor blockade arriving just as you stand up. Twenty minutes keeps you out of deep sleep, so you don't wake in a hole. This is a rescue manoeuvre for the last third of a hard shift, the drive home in an emergency, and exam-level weeks. Done nightly, it becomes a debt and it steals your anchor nap.

## Withdrawal is why people think they need it

If you take 400 mg daily and skip the 03:00 cup, you may get a headache, nausea and a brain-fog that peaks around 12–24 hours after the last dose — right in the middle of your next shift. That withdrawal headache feels exactly like "proof" that the coffee is necessary. It's proof that the body has adapted.

So: when you cut, cut across a week, not overnight, and preferably across a run of *easy* shifts. Drop one instant coffee to half-caf for three days. Delete the fourth cup before deleting the third. The goal isn't a low-caffeine life — it's caffeine that's *placed*, so that by the time you're in bed it's a background hum instead of a bell.

# 5. The Light Curtain and the $0 Bedroom Rebuild

If you change one thing after reading this book, change the bedroom. Light and noise are what actually destroy day sleep, and they are almost always fixable this week for under the cost of a takeaway — often for nothing, from things you already own.

Light first, because it's the lever that moves the clock.

## Why light beats every other intervention

A day worker's bedroom problems are mostly comfort problems: a mattress, a temperature, a partner. Yours are *signal* problems. Bright morning light hitting your retina at 08:00 is an unambiguous instruction to your suprachiasmatic nucleus that it is daytime, and it will override your desire to sleep because it is a survival circuit, not a preference.

Which gives you two facts to design around:

1. **You cannot avoid the drive-home light, so you block it.** Sunglasses on before you leave the building. Not inside the car after you've already had 20 minutes of sky.
2. **The room must be darker than your eyelids.** A "dim" room at 08:00 is, to your clock, daytime. Aim for the level where you genuinely cannot see the wall.

And on the shift side: **10 minutes of bright light in your first hour** is a real, cheap, dose-response alertness intervention with the added benefit of nudging your clock to treat your evening as the start of your day. Every night you skip it, you're fighting the clock instead of moving it. Stand under the corridor lights. Sit by the loading-bay floodlight with your coffee. Walk the perimeter for six minutes. Silly, effective, free.

## The light curtain (no purchase)

**For bed: the towel system.** A rolled-up towel or a draught excluder at the bottom of the door kills the hall light and reduces noise. A blanket or duvet pinned or taped over the window — safety pins through the duvet cover into tape on the wall, or clips on a washing line across the window frame — blocks more light than most cheap "blackout" curtains sold as blackout, because those are usually just lined fabric with side gaps. The gaps are the real problem: light entering around the edge at pillow height is what wakes people.

**Aluminium foil, matte side out, taped to the glass.** Ugly. Extremely effective. Zero cost. If you rent and the landlord matters, note that tape residue on glass is easily removed with hot water and a blade, and foil on a window is not a modification most people would call damage. Photograph it on move-in day so you're not arguing about it later.

**Cardboard + a bin bag over a doorway** if you sleep in a living room or your bedroom door can't seal. Sleep in the room with the fewest windows. If you have a hallway cupboard or a box under a bed frame with a duvet over it, people in shared housing do in fact build these, and they work.

**Eye mask.** If you can spend anything, this is the item with the highest ratio of effect to cost in the entire book: a proper 3D-cup mask (the kind that doesn't press on the lids) beats any curtain for shared housing, shifts and travel. It is also the item people won't wear because of a partner, which is the actual blocker — so say the sentence out loud: *"this isn't about you, this is about my brain."*

**Amber glasses, 1 hour before bed.** Indoor blue-blocking glasses with amber lenses are a modest tool, not a magic one — the evidence in real-world use is weaker than the marketing. If you're finishing a shift at 07:00 in daylight, glasses do nothing for the outdoors anyway. Their genuine use is the last hour before bed *indoors*: the 06:00 handover, the packing-up, the drive. Cheap versions on a marketplace are fine; don't buy a premium pair.

## Noise, temperature, and the things nobody mentions

**Noise.** A lawnmower at 07:00, kids at the bus stop, the recycling lorry, a partner's shower. You cannot control a household's daytime, so you mask it. Options in cost order: earplugs (foam, inserted by rolling thin and pulling the ear up — most people insert them wrong and conclude they don't work); a fan in the corner, which masks and cools; an old phone with a rain or fan loop playing through a cheap speaker, timer off, volume just above the street; a white-noise machine is exactly that with worse speakers. The target is not silence — it's a steady background floor that swamps transients. Full silence plus a doorstep conversation wakes you; steady noise does not.

**Temperature.** Aim for roughly 18–20 °C / 65–68 °F in the room, cooler rather than warmer, and the reason is physiological: falling asleep requires your core temperature to fall. Summer is the hard case. Free options: freeze a water bottle and put it behind the fan; a damp sheet over you in a draught; sleep with fewer clothes than feels sensible; if you have a ground-floor room or a hallway tile, sleep lower — heat rises, and people do in fact move their day sleep to the floor of the coolest room in a heatwave. A wet towel over the neck works. A hot shower 90 minutes before bed works counterintuitively by dumping heat through your extremities afterwards.

**Smells and meals.** Not a comfort issue: your brain keeps monitoring a full stomach, and the 3-2-1-0 countdown's "no food 3 hours before" exists partly because of reflux. If you have reflux, the fix is the timing and the head-of-bed elevation, not milk.

**Clocks.** Turn the display away, or cover it. The 3 a.m. calculation of hours remaining is the single most effective insomnia-generating behaviour known, and it is entirely voluntary. If you need the clock to wake, one phone across the room is enough.

## The 07:30 pre-sleep routine

Ten minutes, before you're in the door, done in this order:

1. Draw curtains / switch on the foil-and-blanket setup *before you need to sleep*.
2. Fan or window shut, heating off, water bottle filled and on the bedside.
3. Alarm set for lights-out *and* for the anchor nap later. Both, now, while you're still upright.
4. Phone charging outside the room if the household tolerates it; otherwise silent, face down, across the room.
5. Food for the 12:30 wake — a banana and a yoghurt in the fridge by the bedside. If you wake at 13:00 and have to invent lunch, you'll eat sugar and go back to bed.
6. Then shower and eat the small final snack *before* you get in bed, not after — the shower is the pre-sleep cue you want, and getting out of a warm bed to go do it is how 08:10 becomes 09:20.

This is not a ritual for its own sake. Every item removes one decision from the exhausted version of you who arrives home at 07:40.

---

**Do this now:** tonight, in the room where you sleep during the day, spend 12 minutes and roll a towel to the bottom of the door and put a duvet over the window. Don't buy anything, don't research curtains, don't plan. Twelve minutes of manual labour, then sleep in it tomorrow and notice the difference. That observation is what will make you keep it.

# 6. The Reentry — ninety minutes after you clock out

The last 30 minutes of a night shift determine the next 6 hours more than the first 6 hours do. And the most dangerous 20 minutes of your entire week are in here too.

## First: the drive

Roughly one in five serious collisions involves some element of drowsiness, and the peak times for sleep-related crashes are the small hours and mid-afternoon — which is precisely when night workers are commuting. Drowsy driving is impaired driving; the reaction-time and judgment effects are similar, and unlike alcohol there is no test, no gradual awareness, and no way to negotiate with it. You also cannot reliably self-assess sleepiness. That's the whole problem: the part of the brain that would notice is the part that's asleep.

Non-negotiables:

- **Two or more consecutive shifts with under ~10 hours total sleep? Do not drive.** Options that cost nothing: sleep in the car in a safe public place for 20 minutes *then* drive; ask a colleague for a swap of a lift for one morning; public transport; leave the car at work overnight and drive home after sleep; call whoever you'd call when you've been drinking. If none of those exist and you have a family to feed, that's a real and common bind — which is exactly why chapter 7's roster conversation is not optional decoration.
- **If you must drive and you know you're impaired:** 200 mg caffeine, then a 20-minute nap in the car park, then drive with the window down and the radio on. That is a *mitigation*, not a fix, buys maybe 30–45 minutes, and ends abruptly.
- **Bright sunlight on the drive home is a wakefulness signal.** Sunglasses are not a style choice in this context, they are part of your sleep intervention.
- **Do not stop for coffee at 07:20.** That's dose 3, at 40 minutes before bed, and it is the single most common self-inflicted wound on this list. Get water and drive.

The 20-minute rule: if you notice any of the standard tells — drifting within the lane, missing the exit you take every week, eyelids heavy at lights, no memory of the last mile — you pull over immediately, somewhere safe, alarm set, eyes closed. Being 25 minutes late to bed costs you nothing. The alternative is a bad way to end a book about sleep.

## Second: don't do the errands

The reentry mistake is *productive* behaviour. You get home at 07:45 and think: I'll just put the washing on, take the bin out, book the dentist, answer that one text, then sleep.

Two hours later you're on the sofa, still in your uniform, half-asleep but not sleeping, having eaten cereal standing up, now unable to sleep properly because you've lost the window and the pressure has been partially bled off by dozing. Then you sleep 11:00–15:00 and can't sleep before the *next* shift at all. One errand at 07:50 has cost you two nights.

**The rule: from the front door to the bed, nothing that isn't moving toward the bed.** Errands are for the *afternoon* block, after core sleep — you'll be sharper, faster, and the shops are emptier at 14:00 anyway. Write it on your hand if needed. The bin can wait until 13:00. The washing can wait until 13:00. The text message can wait until 13:00.

## Third: the 90-minute descent, in order

| When | Do | Not |
|---|---|---|
| 07:45 | Arrive, sunglasses off, curtains already black. Go straight to shower. | "Just check my phone for a second" on the sofa. |
| 07:55 | Warm shower, 4 minutes. Get *out* of work clothes. | A 25-minute shower that delays bed. |
| 08:05 | Small final snack: yoghurt, eggs, toast and cheese, whatever's already out. Nothing new made. A glass of water. | Cereal and sugar; cooking; "I'll skip food and be hungry" (you'll wake at 3 hours). |
| 08:15 | Alarm for the anchor nap set. Phone away. Room cool. | Setting "sleep as long as I can." |
| 08:20 | Bed. 3-2-1-0 already satisfied if you followed chapters 3–5. | Doing the hours arithmetic. If awake 20 min: out of bed, dim light, boring, return when sleepy. |
| 12:30–13:00 | Wake to alarm, even if it went badly. Light on immediately, 10 minutes by a window or outside. | Sleeping to 15:30 "to catch up," which destroys the next shift's descent. |

That last line is the most important in the chapter. **The wake time is the anchor, not the bedtime.** A bad night's sleep still ends at 12:30, and the anchor nap that evening is slightly longer to compensate. People who instead let a bad night slide their wake to 16:00 end up unable to nap at 16:00, unable to sleep after the shift, and awake for 30 hours by the second night. The wake time is the one number you protect even when it feels like you're punishing yourself. It's the opposite: it's what makes the next night fixable.

## If you can't sleep at all

Occasionally you will get home wired — a bad incident on shift, a phone call, adrenaline. Getting 3 hours is fine. In that order:

1. Don't try. Out of the room, dim light, something boring and physical (fold the laundry, tidy one shelf).
2. Re-enter at a fixed time with an alarm for 4 hours set in advance.
3. If you sleep <2 hours before a shift, do the 90-minute nap at 15:30 and 18:30 both, and reduce the driving risk to zero for that day.
4. Do not "make up" for it with a 5-coffee day. That converts one bad sleep night into four.
5. And no, alcohol is not a sleep aid here. See chapter 3.

If nights of zero sleep are more than occasional, or you're snoring and waking gasping, or you're low-mood most days, that's not a systems problem and this book is the wrong tool — see a clinician. Chapter 1's disclaimer exists because that genuinely happens to a lot of shift workers, and the fix isn't a better eye mask.

---

**Do this now:** decide, tonight, the one thing on your reentry list that is not actually necessary at 07:50. The bin. The dishwasher. The text. Move it to 13:30 and see whether the day changes.

# 7. Days Off, the Bridge Day, and Getting a Better Roster

Everything before this chapter is individual. This one is about two things you can't do alone: days off, and other people's decisions about your hours.

## The two-step pivot

The day-off problem: you finish Wednesday at 07:30, and Thursday is a day off, and everyone tells you to "just switch back." So you try to be normal on Friday and Saturday, which means being awake and miserable on the one Thursday you have with your family, then wrecking the Thursday-night sleep you need for the Friday shift anyway.

Full flips rarely work on 4-on-4-off. What works better is a **two-step pivot**: move your sleep in two ~3-hour steps across the transition, and protect the transition day as *nothing*, rather than as a day with a plan in it.

Example, 4 nights finishing 07:30, then 4 off:

| Day | Sleep | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Thu | 08:00–12:30 core + 16:00–17:30 nap | Normal, but the nap is shortened to 90 min and the evening is protected for the pivot. |
| **Fri (bridge)** | 09:00–14:00 (one hour later than Thursday, then straight through) | **The bridge day.** No plans requiring energy before 16:00. No driving after 20:00. This day exists to absorb the shift, not to be used. |
| Sat | 23:00–07:30 | First "normal" night. You'll be genuinely tired; that's the mechanism. |
| Sun | 23:00–08:00 | Consolidate. Social things happen now, evenings are yours. |
| Mon | 22:30–07:00 | Back to a day rhythm. |
| Tue | shift starts again — reverse: 01:00–06:30 core + 14:00–15:30 nap | Pivot *back* is easier because you only need to be awake for 12 hours. |

Three rules make this work:

1. **One step per day, never all at once.** An hour or two of bedtime shift per day is sustainable; a 9-hour jump is a night of lying awake.
2. **The bridge day is scheduled as empty.** Errands, admin, social stuff after 16:00. On the bridge day you are the person who is "a bit out of it" and that's fine, but you should not be the person driving at 22:00 or cooking a family meal or attending a birthday.
3. **Morning light does the heavy lifting on the pivot days.** On the two days you're stepping later, get 15–20 minutes of outdoor light as early as you can tolerate it, and dim the house after 21:00. That's a bigger effect than any single trick in chapter 5, because you're aiming it at a specific target date rather than maintaining.

If your pattern is 3-on-3-off or 2-on-2-off, the bridge day often doesn't exist — and you should not attempt a full daily flip on a 2-off pattern. On that roster, staying nocturnal through the days off is often the *less* damaging option, which horrifies families and is frequently the right call. Fewer total transitions, less whiplash, more consistent light exposure. The 28-day log will tell you which of the two you are; people who try both report a strong preference fast.

## The calendar conversation nobody has

Most household damage from night work isn't tiredness. It's invisible scheduling. Fix that with a shared, visible calendar and two sentences:

> My sleep window is 08:00 to 12:30 on shift days. Anything that needs me — school runs, appointments, lifts — put it after 13:00.
> On my bridge day I'm not much use. Don't plan anything that needs a driver or a decision-maker before 16:00.

Then put the *roster itself* on the fridge, in colour, for the next 4 weeks. Not on your phone where nobody else looks. A printed roster with "SLEEP" as a highlighted block is how you stop being woken at 10:15 for a thing that could have been asked at 14:00, and it's how your family stops treating your day as free time.

## Getting a better roster: the actual script

Shift-swapping, sick absence, and burnout turnover cost employers real money. That's your leverage, and it's more than you think. But you have to ask in a way that survives a rota manager's inbox, which means: written, specific, low-cost to say yes to, with a fallback that costs them nothing.

Do not ask: *"Can you change my shift pattern, I'm knackered."* That's a problem handed to someone else with no solution in it, and it gets a "we'll see" that means no.

Do ask, by email so there's a record:

> **Subject: Request — fixed block of 4 nights, or 2 late-start shifts added**
>
> Hi [name],
>
> I'd like to request a change to my rotation for health and safety reasons relating to shift-work recovery.
>
> **What I'm asking for:** moving from my current mixed rotation (2 lates, 3 nights, 1 early across a fortnight) to a fixed block of 4 nights with 4 off, with the early-shift cover I currently do reassigned.
>
> **Why:** the early after three nights is where I'm least safe on the commute and least effective at handover. Forward-rotation and consistent blocks are what occupational guidance recommends for exactly this reason.
>
> **What it costs the rota:** nothing — I'll keep my total hours and I'm willing to take the Fri/Sat night slots nobody wants in exchange, and I can absorb two extra shifts a month in the fortnights where the block would break.
>
> **If that's not possible:** the smaller version is removing the single early shift that follows three nights, and giving me two consecutive light-duty days after a 3-night run.
>
> Happy to talk for five minutes whenever suits. [Your name, staff no., date]

Why that works: it names the specific change, quantifies the cost as zero, offers something back, provides a fallback that's easier to grant, frames it in safety language that a manager has to respond to seriously, and creates a paper trail if it's refused.

A few practical notes:

- **Ask for the smaller version too.** Requests with an easy fallback get a yes on *something*; requests with one option get a no.
- **Attach the evidence only if it exists.** A letter from a clinician about shift-work-related sleep problems converts a preference into an adjustment consideration in many workplaces. I'm not going to tell you to get one you don't need — but if you have a real sleep disorder, that's what a clinician is for, and employers respond to paperwork differently than to fatigue.
- **Use the safety frame, not the complaint frame.** Rotas move on risk language. "Least safe on the commute" gets read; "I'm exhausted" gets sympathy and no change.
- **Collect allies.** A request from three people in one department is a rota problem; from one it's a personal preference.
- **If the answer is no:** ask, in writing, for the reason and for what conditions would make it yes. That sentence changes behaviour in a way that arguing doesn't.

## The 4-on-4-off-specific advice

If you can choose, take nights in a block of 3 or 4 with rotation *forwards* (days → evenings → nights), never backwards, and never an early shift after nights. Cap consecutive nights at 4. Ask for the first night off after a block to be the day you don't drive anywhere after 20:00. Sleep experts and occupational guidance agree on these points far more than they agree on anything else, which is why they're worth spending political capital on.

Everything else in this book is a way to survive a rota. This chapter is the only one that might change it. Don't skip it because it's the least comfortable — it usually has the biggest single effect on people's lives, and it costs one email.

---

**Do this now:** identify your next bridge day, put "nothing before 16:00" in the calendar, and copy the roster request above into a draft email tonight. Do not send it yet. Just let it exist as a real option. Most people who write the draft send it within 10 days, and most of those get at least the fallback.

# 8. The 28-Day Protocol

One rule added per day, one number logged per morning. Four weeks. By day 28 you have a system tuned to your body rather than a book's guesses about the average body.

## Setup (do this once, 15 minutes)

- Print or photocopy the log grid in chapter 9, 28 copies, or use the free web planner's day view.
- Decide your **target lights-out** for each shift type. Write it down. It cannot change mid-week except for illness.
- Take a baseline photo of your bedroom as-is. You'll want it later.
- Tell one person: "I'm doing a 28-day thing with my sleep, and if I'm a monster on day 3, that's why."

## The four weeks

**Week 1 — anchor.** Only two changes. They are timing, not effort.

| Day | Add |
|---|---|
| 1 | Set the 3-2-1-0 alarms: last food at T−3, last caffeine at T−2, lights down at T−1. Nothing else. |
| 2 | Anchor meal in the first hour of shift, with 30 g protein. |
| 3 | Log caffeine in mg with times. Do not reduce. |
| 4 | Cap total at 400 mg. |
| 5 | Second dose by 23:30 (or T−6 for your start), then stop. |
| 6 | Descent list written and followed: nap, light, food, coffee, bag. |
| 7 | Review: what felt easy, what felt impossible. Keep only what was easy. |

**Week 2 — the room.**

| Day | Add |
|---|---|
| 8 | Towel at the door, duvet or foil on the window. |
| 9 | Earplugs, correctly inserted, or fan on. |
| 10 | Room at 18–20 °C; heating off before bed, not after. |
| 11 | Clock turned away or covered. |
| 12 | The 07:30 pre-sleep routine, all six steps, before you're in the door. |
| 13 | Sunglasses on before leaving the building; nothing but bed-movement after arriving. |
| 14 | Review: rate sleep onset latency and 06:00 energy against week 1. |

**Week 3 — structure.**

| Day | Add |
|---|---|
| 15 | Anchor nap at the same time every shift, ≤90 min, alarm on. |
| 16 | Wake time after core sleep fixed, even after a bad night. |
| 17 | Snack ladder written, printed, photographed. Tier A only. |
| 18 | 10 minutes of bright light in the first hour of shift. |
| 19 | 10 minutes of movement at hour 6 before reaching for food. |
| 20 | Bridge day protected, nothing before 16:00. |
| 21 | Review: total sleep per 24h, not per night. This is the number that matters. |

**Week 4 — calibration.**

| Day | Add |
|---|---|
| 22 | Change **one** number: protein at anchor meal (up if hungry, down if reflux), or lights-out ±30 min. |
| 23 | Try the coffee nap once, on the hardest night. |
| 24 | Test split vs. block sleep deliberately for one day; pick a preference. |
| 25 | Draft the roster email from chapter 7. |
| 26 | One week of no alcohol after nights, if you drink. Compare. |
| 27 | Send the email, or decide not to and write down the real reason. |
| 28 | Full review below. Rebook the same calendar for the next 28 days. |

## The weekly review, in 20 minutes

Answer in writing, every day 7, 14, 21, 28:

1. **Total sleep per 24h** across the week (core + naps). Is it above 6? Above 6.5?
2. **Onset latency:** how many nights did it take >30 minutes?
3. **The 06:00 rating** (1–5). The end-of-shift number is the honest one.
4. **Which rule did I actually break on which day, and what was on that day?** Usually it's the same day of the week, the same shift, or the same person.
5. **Which rule cost nothing and worked?** Promote it to permanent.
6. **Which rule cost something and didn't work?** Delete it. No guilt. Deleting rules is progress.

Then change exactly one thing for the following week.

## What "success" looks like at day 28

Not: eight hours a night, energetic, roster fixed. That's not what this is. Realistic:

- Falling asleep within 30 minutes on most shift-mornings instead of two hours.
- Total sleep per 24 hours up by 60–90 minutes, mostly from naps you didn't take before.
- The 04:00 crash less steep, because of where the anchor meal and the light went.
- Zero drowsy driving, which is the outcome that matters more than the other three combined.
- A personal caffeine cut-off in hours, measured, not guessed.
- Some number of rules you deleted because they didn't fit. That's a feature of doing this properly.

If after 28 days you have 6 good days a week instead of 2, the system is working. If nothing at all moved, that's information about a sleep or health issue that a book can't fix, and the right next step is a clinician rather than another month of trying harder.

**Do this now:** day 1 is one alarm for "last food." Not the whole protocol. One alarm, tonight.
