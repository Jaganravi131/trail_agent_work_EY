# Caffeine on a Night Shift: the Half-Life Math Nobody Does for You

![A break-room desk at night: a mug, a phone showing 03:12, and a notebook with times written down](images/breakroom-coffee-3am.jpg)

Caffeine is the most useful thing on a night shift and the most common reason you're awake at 08:00 staring at the ceiling. Both facts come from the same property: it lasts a long time, and it does nothing about the tiredness itself.

That's the whole mental model. **Caffeine doesn't reduce sleep pressure — it blocks the receptors that let you feel it.** The pressure stays banked, and it collects with interest when the block wears off. So caffeine isn't an energy source at all. It's a *timing tool*. Placed deliberately, it's excellent. Used as a substitute for sleep, it's a loan at a bad rate.

## What's in your blood, hour by hour

Elimination half-life in a healthy non-smoking adult is commonly given around **5 hours**, with a real range of roughly 2–10: faster if you smoke, considerably slower in pregnancy, and altered by some medications (several common ones — certain antibiotics, some antidepressants, some contraceptives — can slow caffeine clearance noticeably, which is a pharmacist conversation, not a guess).

Start from **200 mg at 02:00** — a large café coffee, or a 500 ml energy drink:

| Clock | Roughly still active | What it feels like |
|---|---|---|
| 02:30 | 195 mg | Good. This is the point of it. |
| 07:00 | 128 mg | Fine at work. Comforting, even. |
| 08:00, in bed | **100 mg** | "Tired but wired." |
| 12:00 | 50 mg | A small coffee's worth, while you're in bed. |
| 17:00 | 25 mg | Not zero. |

That's a single dose. Now remember the day had five.

This is why "I can fall asleep fine after coffee" is not evidence. Falling asleep is one component of sleep, and it's the least sensitive one. Caffeine reduces deep sleep and increases light sleep in people who report no difficulty nodding off, and it shifts the circadian clock later — which is exactly the thing you're trying not to do. In the study people often cite on this, 400 mg given 0, 3 **and 6 hours** before bedtime all produced measurable disruption, including the 6-hour condition where subjects *felt* unaffected. You are not a reliable instrument for measuring your own caffeine. Trust the arithmetic.

## Two doses per shift

For a 19:00–07:00 rotation, with lights-out near 08:30:

- **Dose 1 — 18:50, 100–200 mg, with food.** Timed to the clock, not to the first crisis of the night. Taken with food, so the stomach doesn't get an empty acid bath at 02:00.
- **Dose 2 — between 22:30 and 23:30, ~100 mg.** Covers hours 4–6, the first real slump.
- **Then stop.** Decaf, water, tea from a flask. The rest of the shift is light, movement, protein and a short nap — not chemistry.

If you'd rather have a rule that travels across rosters: **last caffeine at least 2 hours before bed; 6 hours if you can.** Two is the floor. Six is what the data actually supports. For a 12-hour night shift, six hours lands at roughly hour 6, which is the same point as "by 23:30."

## What you're actually drinking

Real values vary enormously — a chain "medium" can be 150 or 300 mg — so treat these as planning numbers, not lab figures.

| Source | Typical size | Caffeine |
|---|---|---|
| Instant, made in the break room | 250 ml mug | 60–90 mg |
| Drip / filter | 350 ml | 120–200 mg |
| Espresso | single shot | 50–75 mg |
| Cold brew | 470 ml | 180–280 mg |
| Black tea, 3 min | 250 ml | 40–70 mg |
| Green tea | 250 ml | 20–45 mg |
| Cola | 330 ml can | 30–40 mg |
| Energy drink | 250 ml can | ~80 mg |
| Energy drink | 500 ml can | ~160 mg |
| Dark chocolate | 40 g | 20–35 mg |
| Pre-workout | one scoop | 150–300 mg |

The figure food-safety authorities commonly cite for healthy adults is **up to about 400 mg across the day**, with single doses around 200 mg. A ceiling, not a target, and not applicable to pregnancy (where the widely quoted figure is roughly half or less), adolescence, or with certain cardiac, anxiety or medication histories. If any of those apply, that's a pharmacist or clinician question — no blog post, including this one, should be answering it.

Two things blow through the ceiling on night shifts without anyone noticing: a pre-workout scoop at 17:00 (200 mg) followed by four coffees, and "I'll just have another of these" energy drink at 03:00. Write down every milligram for one shift and you'll almost certainly find one dose to delete.

## The coffee nap (the one trick worth borrowing)

The single place timing beats amount, and it costs nothing:

1. **200 mg, fast** — a coffee, a can, or a tablet (the cheapest dose-per-unit anywhere and the only accurate one).
2. **Immediately lie down or recline, eyes closed, alarm at 20 minutes.**
3. **Wake as the caffeine starts to peak.**

You bank the sleep-pressure reduction from the nap and the receptor blockade arrives as you stand up. Twenty minutes keeps you out of deep sleep, so you don't wake in a hole.

Use it for the last third of a hard shift and for the drive home in an emergency. **Do not use it nightly** — it borrows from your anchor nap, and nightly borrowing compounds.

## Withdrawal is why people think they need it

Cut the 03:00 cup and you may get a headache, nausea and fog that typically begins 12–24 hours after the last dose — squarely in the middle of a shift. That headache feels like proof the coffee is necessary. It's proof of adaptation.

So taper across a week, ideally over a run of *easy* shifts: drop a brew to half-caf for three days, delete the fourth cup before the third, keep dose 1 untouched. And keep the two-dose structure even if the doses get smaller — a scheduled 150 mg is more useful to you than an unscheduled 200 mg reached for three times.

The goal isn't a low-caffeine life. It's caffeine that's *placed*, so that at lights-out it's a background hum instead of a bell.
