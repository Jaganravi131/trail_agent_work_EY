# Sleep Before a Night Shift: the 90-Minute Nap, the 20-Minute Nap, and When Neither Works

![A bedroom at 15:40 with blackout tape on the window edges, a fan running, an alarm clock face-down on the nightstand and shoes already on by the door](images/pre-shift-nap.jpg)
*Alt text is the actual image description here — replace with a real photo of your own setup. Everything in this page works the same whether the room is handsome or held together with tape.*

Most advice about pre-shift sleep stops at "get a good night's rest," which is useless at 15:40 on a Tuesday when you have 4.5 hours in the tank, a child to collect at 16:15 and a shift that starts at 19:00. The real questions are arithmetic: how much, when, and which of the two nap types you're actually able to use today.

This is the pre-shift half of the problem. The post-shift half — the wired-at-08:00 thing — is [here](/sleep-after-night-shift/).

## The two numbers that decide the whole day

Two forces compete every time you try to sleep, in different directions:

**Sleep pressure** builds while you're awake. It's roughly a debt you repay at a fixed rate, and it's what lets you drop off in the first place. Four hours after a full day of being up, you have a lot of it. Twelve hours after, you have almost none — which is why a long sleep the wrong side of the anchor makes the next one harder, not easier.

**Your body clock** is a separate signal, set almost entirely by light at the eye, and it has a preferred window for sleep. For a night worker who has partially adapted, that window drifts later. For one who hasn't, it's still pointed at 03:00–05:00 in the morning — meaning 15:40 is close to the *worst* time on your clock to try to fall asleep, even though your pressure is high.

Sleep before a shift works when those two lines cross. It fails, night after night, when people treat one of them as the whole problem and buy blackout curtains to fix a clock problem, or go to bed earlier and earlier to fix a pressure problem.

## The floor: 4.5 hours of core, 1.5 hours of nap

The number worth protecting is not eight. For a 12-hour night, the working floor in this system is:

- **4.5 hours of anchored core sleep**, ending at a consistent time, in a room you've made dark and cool.
- **1.5 hours of nap**, roughly one full sleep cycle, taken 60–150 minutes before the shift starts.

That's six hours. It is not plenty, and no page that tells you it is plenty deserves your bookmark. It is enough to be functional and to keep the mistake rate flat through hour eleven, and — this is the part nobody mentions — it is *repeatable* on a family schedule. A plan that requires 8 uninterrupted hours before every night is a plan you will abandon by week two.

If you only get the core and not the nap, you'll be tired for the first third of the shift and usually fine after. If you only get the nap, you'll be fine until about 04:00 and then you'll make a decision you're glad nobody was watching.

## Which nap: 20 minutes or 90, and never the middle

Nap length matters because of *when you wake up*, not how long you slept.

- **20 minutes** lands before deep sleep properly starts. You wake with something in hand, and you're lucid inside five minutes. It's the nap for a shift you have to be sharp on immediately — safety-critical work, driving a vehicle, anything where you'll be operating machinery in the first hour.
- **90 minutes** is close to a full cycle: light sleep, deep sleep, REM, out the other side. It does far more for a long shift and for your mood on day three of four, and if the alarm lands as the cycle finishes, you wake reasonably clean.
- **30–60 minutes is the bad zone.** You fall asleep around minute ten, so a 45-minute alarm catches you in deep sleep, and you wake with sleep inertia — objectively worse reaction time for anything up to an hour. A 45-minute nap before a 19:00 start can make the first two hours of your shift worse than not napping.

So the honest decision rule: if you can give yourself 90 uninterrupted minutes plus a 10-minute buffer, take the long nap. If you can give yourself 25 minutes, set the alarm for 20 and take the short one. If you can give yourself 50, don't nap — lie down, no alarm, eyes closed, and get up at 30. Quiet rest with the lights low buys you a surprising amount and costs you no inertia.

## The worked version for a 19:00–07:00 shift

Times, not vibes, because a nap is scheduled or it doesn't happen:

| Time | What | Why that time |
|---|---|---|
| 08:30 | asleep | ~90 minutes after finishing the drive home and the food; see the post-shift page for the countdown that gets you there |
| 13:00 | up, lights on, 10 minutes of bright light | this is the anchor. Shift it and the whole table moves and nothing improves |
| 13:20 | 200 mg caffeine, with food | ~5 hours of useful coverage, and it's the *only* dose you need before work |
| 15:45 | nap alarm, 16:00 asleep | one cycle, ending before you need to be functional |
| 17:30 | up, face in light | 30–60 minutes of inertia burns off before you leave; don't drive in it |
| 17:50 | 30 g protein, real food | eating at 16:50 instead leaves you peaked at the wrong end |
| 18:50 | out the door; daylight-free commute if the sun is up | last light exposure of the day should be your commute *home*, not here |
| 23:30 | last caffeine of the shift | five hours before 04:30, which is roughly where your wall is |
| 05:30 | food stops | a heavy meal at 05:15 is a 08:30 gut-noise wake-up |
| 07:30 | lights on for the drive home | deliberately the one bright moment of your morning |
| 08:30 | asleep again | the loop closes |

The part people skip is 13:00. The wake time is what sets tonight's nap and tomorrow's sleep; the bedtime is a consequence. Chase bedtime and you'll be in bed at 22:00 staring at a ceiling fan you own for exactly this purpose.

Run your own numbers through [the planner](/planner/) rather than copying my table — start and finish times, days off, and whether you're rotating change the clock times even though they don't change the mechanism.

## "I can't nap on demand" — the two-week part nobody puts in the tips list

Napping is a skill with a practice schedule, not a switch. Most people who say they can't nap are saying they've tried seven times in a strange room under time pressure. What moves the needle:

1. **Same room, same sequence, alarm set, and you are not allowed to succeed.** For ten days the goal is lying still with eyes closed at 15:45, not sleeping. You're teaching the body that this time and this room mean down-regulation. Most people start dropping off in three to six days, and several report the first *actual* nap arriving on day eight out of nowhere.
2. **Do not nap in the bed you sleep in at 08:30.** A chair, a couch, even a car seat in a driveway. The core bed needs to stay tied to the core sleep; a nap that wrecks your night's sleep is a net loss.
3. **Keep the alarm across the room.** Waking at 17:30 and hitting snooze into 18:15 puts you squarely in the inertia window with 45 minutes to get to work. That's how a good nap becomes a bad shift.

## When a nap is the wrong tool

Three cases where napping before the shift makes the week worse:

- **You can't sleep afterwards.** If the pre-shift nap reliably costs you the 08:30 sleep, the nap is borrowing from the only long block you have. Take the 20-minute version or drop it, and protect the core.
- **Your shift is irregular enough that you can't hold a wake time.** Anchor sleep needs an anchor. With no consistent start, work on the post-shift countdown and the light rules first — those don't depend on the roster cooperating.
- **You're doing it to compensate for a crash you should not be driving through.** If you've had two hours and the shift is four hours away, the safe answer is boring: call in, don't drive, do not use a nap as a licence to get behind a wheel drowsy. You cannot reliably self-assess sleepiness, because the bit of the brain that would notice is the bit that's asleep.

And, still the rule that overrides everything on this site: **never drive when drowsy.** Caffeine and 20 minutes buy you roughly half an hour of mitigation, not a fix.

## When to stop optimising and get it looked at

A nap plan is for a tired person with a normal physiology problem. Stop self-managing and see a clinician if:

- You snore, wake gasping, or you're unrefreshed no matter how much you get — sleep apnoea is badly under-diagnosed in shift populations, and no nap treats it.
- You cannot sleep at all in a dark, cool room at 08:30, on any day of the week, for weeks.
- You're dozing off unintentionally during the day, or at the wheel.
- Your mood is low most days, or things you liked have gone flat.
- The sleep problem started when a medication or a roster change started — that's a different conversation with a different fix.
- You're considering any supplement or drug for timing. Ask a pharmacist or clinician before you take it, and specifically ask about interactions and timing against your own medication list. This page doesn't give dosing plans for anything, on purpose.
