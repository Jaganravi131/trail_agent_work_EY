# What to Eat on a Night Shift: the Anchor-Meal Rule (and 4 Menus for Real Break Rooms)

![An open fridge with five identical containers of chicken, potatoes and peppers, and a flask on top](images/batch-cook-five-containers.jpg)

Supermarkets are designed for the evening shop. Diet plans are designed for lunch. Break rooms are designed for a 12:30 bell you aren't on. So you improvise with a vending machine at 03:30, eat the crackers, and conclude you have no discipline.

You don't have a discipline problem. You have a *timing* problem, plus an environment that was never built for you. Fix the timing and the food gets easy.

## The anchor-meal rule: your big meal goes in hour one

Most night workers eat backwards. They arrive not hungry, graze all evening, then eat the only real meal at 03:00 or 04:00 because that's when the break finally materialises — and then come home at 07:30 too full or too refluxy to sleep, and too hungry at 05:00 to focus.

Flip it. **Eat the main meal within the first hour of your shift**, hot, with protein. Then graze small, savoury things through the biological night, with the last food about 3 hours before you plan to sleep.

The reasoning is straightforward physiology. Digestion is under circadian control: glucose handling and gastric emptying are measurably worse during the biological night than during the day, which is one reason night-eating patterns show up in the literature alongside adverse metabolic outcomes in long-term shift workers. You can't override that with willpower, but you can stop testing it at 04:00. A large meal also raises core temperature and starts a digestive process that takes hours — two things actively opposed to falling asleep at 08:00.

**The number: about 30 g of protein at that meal.** Not gym-culture filler. Protein is the most reliable lever for actually feeling fed for six hours, and a typical break-room "meal" — sandwich plus a biscuit, or a ready meal — lands at 14–18 g, which is exactly why you're hungry again at 02:00. Add one thing: a cheese string, a carton of milk, two boiled eggs, a scoop of protein powder stirred into yoghurt, a tin of tuna on the side. That's +15 g for no cooking.

## Three decisions, not forty

Don't try to eat well on a night shift. Make three decisions well, the day before, on paper.

**1. Anchor meal, first hour.** See the menus below.

**2. A snack ladder you never re-decide on.** Written at 18:40, photographed as your lock screen or taped inside a locker, ranked by what you'll actually eat:

| Tier | Option | Protein | Prep |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Yoghurt tube + cheese string | ~18 g | none |
| A | Salted nuts + UHT milk carton | ~12 g | none |
| A | Two boiled eggs | ~12 g | 10 min, once a week |
| B | Protein bar — pick ≥15 g protein and <8 g sugars | 15–20 g | none |
| B | Tuna pouch + crackers | ~20 g | 30 seconds |
| C | Vending: peanuts > crisps > biscuits | 5–9 g | coins |
| D | Unclaimed break-room leftovers | 0 g | shame |

The point isn't optimality, it's **decision removal**. A decision you already made doesn't have to be re-made by the exhausted version of you at 03:00, who will lose every time to a doughnut.

**3. Fluids.** Roughly a litre over a 12-hour shift in air conditioning, more if you're on your feet in heat. A 03:00 headache on a night shift is very often dehydration plus mild caffeine withdrawal wearing a coat, and a carton of milk with salted nuts fixes both.

## Hour six: the slump, and why food is the wrong lever

Around hour 6 (roughly 01:00 on a 19:00 start), and again around hour 9–10, you hit the circadian floor. Reaction time drops, and food becomes psychologically irresistible because eating *feels* like an intervention.

Mostly it isn't one. A biscuit is 40 minutes of glucose and 90 minutes of worse fatigue. In descending order of effect per unit of effort:

1. **10 minutes of bright light.** Walk to the lit yard, stand by the open freezer door, put your face near the corridor LEDs. Free, fast, and the only lever that actually nudges the clock.
2. **10 minutes of movement.** Stairs, a lap of the building, marching in a stairwell. You're raising core temperature, which is what the slump is a fall *in*.
3. **Cold water on face and wrists.**
4. **A conversation with another human.** Alertness is socially upregulated. The colleague who chats for four minutes is measurably more awake than the one who eats a second doughnut.
5. **15–20 minutes eyes closed, alarm on** — if your role and your employer's rules allow it. The most underrated tool in shift work, and the one people feel most ridiculous using.
6. **Then** food.

If you need sugar, have it *with* protein and fat (peanuts and a cola beats a cola), which flattens the slope rather than removing it.

## Four menus by real constraint

### One microwave, a 20-minute break, a shared fridge
Batch on your day off into five portions: ~1.5 kg chicken thighs, a roast tray of potatoes and peppers, a tin of white beans. Anchor meal = two portions plus a cheese string and an apple. Snack = the beans with a boiled egg. **Cost per shift is whatever your normal dinner costs**, because you're eating your dinner at 18:00 instead of 20:00. That's the whole trick — there's no new shopping list and no "meal prep lifestyle" required.

### No microwave, no fridge
The no-cool-bag stack: UHT milk cartons (200 ml, ~7 g protein each), tuna or salmon pouches, peanuts in the shell, oatcakes, bread rolls, string cheese (fine for 4 hours in a bag), bananas. That's ~35 g protein per meal with no equipment and no refrigeration. Add a flask of hot water: a warm drink at 03:00 is doing psychological work far beyond its zero calories. Full list here → [no-microwave night shift meal prep](/no-microwave-meal-prep/).

### An 8-minute break that may not come
Edible in one hand while working, if that's your reality: a chicken-and-slaw wrap (~25 g), a yoghurt pouch, a shake from a carton. And when a real break *does* land, spend it sitting still and looking at something far away instead of eating. At hour 9, rest often beats nutrition and you'll be surprised by the difference.

### Long shifts and back-to-back nights
On a run of three or more, don't chase "well." Chase **identical**: the same anchor meal at the same time each shift. Variety is a daytime luxury; novelty costs decision energy. Batch the boring thing.

## Two things to stop doing

**Alcohol after shift.** Two units with the sunrise on the window is not a wind-down. Alcohol reliably shortens the second half of a sleep period even when it makes the first half faster, and on 4 hours of sleep that converts into a shift where you are slower than you believe you are. The cheapest experiment in this topic: one week of tea and a snack after nights, compared against your own log from the week before. For a lot of people this changes sleep more than any blackout setup.

**Skipping the anchor meal because you're not hungry at 17:50.** Understandable — appetite is genuinely lowest during your biological night. But 900 calories at 04:00 is the alternative, and it's worse on both fronts. So make the anchor meal *small and protein-forward*: three eggs, or yoghurt with protein stirred through, or sardines on toast. 250 calories, 30 g protein, four minutes.

## Getting your own numbers

These are population-level defaults. Your gut at 03:00 is not the average gut in a study. The 2-minute log in [the planner](/planner/) — protein grams at anchor meal, time of last food, how hour 9 felt out of five — is enough to find your own cut-off in about three shifts, which is cheaper than any diet app and won't try to sell you anything.
